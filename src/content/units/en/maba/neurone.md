## In short

An artificial neuron computes a **weighted sum** of its inputs, adds a [[biais|bias]], then applies an [[fonction-activation]]. Geometrically, it cuts space with **one straight line**.

## The biological inspiration

- The brain is made of **separate neurons** (neuron doctrine), linked by [[synapse|synapses]].
- According to [[hebb|Hebb]], learning = **strengthening connections** used together. That's the idea behind a network's [[poids|weights]].

The [[neurone-mp]] computes $w_1x_1 + w_2x_2$ and compares it with the [[seuil|threshold]] $\theta$: output 1 if reached, 0 otherwise. With $w = (1, 1)$: $\theta = 2$ gives AND, $\theta = 1$ gives OR. But the weights are **chosen by hand**: it doesn't learn.

## The perceptron: learned weights

The [[perceptron]] keeps the same structure but **adjusts its weights** from its errors. It's the first machine that learns from data.

- The **weights** set the line's **direction**.
- The **bias** sets its **position**. Without a bias, the line must pass through the origin.

AND and OR are [[lineairement-separable|linearly separable]]: one line is enough. [[xor]] isn't → a single neuron fails. Solution: several neurons in layers → see [[couche-cachee]].

## Pitfalls

- The M-P neuron **doesn't learn**; the perceptron does.
- Bias $= -\theta$ (watch the sign).
- A single neuron can never do XOR, whatever its weights.

## Key takeaways

- Weighted sum + bias + activation.
- Weights = direction, bias = position.
- 1 neuron = 1 line → no XOR.
